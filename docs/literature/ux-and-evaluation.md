# UX and Evaluation Literature for Story to Game Creation

This section informs the story-entry interface, generation feedback, error recovery, and later playtests of the proposed browser game platform. It reviews four distinct scholarly sources, three published in 2023–2026 and one foundational 2019 HCI study. The studies concern different domains, so their findings motivate design hypotheses for our platform; they do not establish that our interface has already been tested.

| Source and type | Approach and evidence | Finding relevant to this project | Limitation | Requirement or evaluation implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. (2023), CHI paper** [1] | Ten non-AI experts used the BotDesigner prototype to design an instructional chatbot by changing and locally testing LLM prompts. Researchers observed their design process. | Participants tended to iterate opportunistically and generalize from one success or failure; some interpreted the model through expectations of human instruction. A free-text story box alone may leave novice authors unsure how their wording affects the game. | Small, academia-skewed sample, limited design time, and chatbot domain. The observed behavior cannot be assumed to generalize directly to game creation. | Keep the story field, but add optional explicit controls for genre, goal, player actions, and constraints. In usability testing, observe whether authors can express these without expert prompting and whether they understand the result of changes. |
| **Kim, Chung and Kim (2026), journal article** [2] | Formative observations/interviews with 16 novices informed a guided text-to-image prompt interface. A separate 16-person comparison against Midjourney used a usability questionnaire and qualitative observations. | Structured categories and visual guidance helped these novices articulate creative intent and perceive more predictable results in image generation. It suggests offering guided choices alongside an editable narrative. | Image generation, controlled reference tasks, one comparator, and small/narrow sample. The paper does not establish the right categories or usability gain for games. | Prototype a form with story, genre, main action, and desired ending, with examples and an optional free-form area. Compare it with plain story entry in a small task-based study; measure completion and ask whether the game preserved the intended premise. |
| **Amershi et al. (2019), CHI paper** [3] | Synthesized more than 150 recommendations into 18 human-AI interaction guidelines, refined through multiple evaluations including 49 practitioners examining 20 AI-infused products. | Relevant guidelines include communicating capability and limitations, enabling correction when AI is wrong, gracefully narrowing uncertain behavior, and explaining why a result occurred. | Broad AI-product heuristics, developed before modern story-to-game generators; the study does not measure our particular users or game's playability. | State supported game mechanics before submission; show progress and failure states; display each simplification with its reason; let authors edit the request and retry rather than losing it. Inspect these states during prototype review. |
| **Kirginas (2023), scholarly book chapter** [4] | Surveys methods for evaluating game user experience across quantitative/qualitative, subjective/objective, formative/summative, and before/during/after-play approaches. | Game experience has multiple dimensions, and a working launch alone cannot show that players understand controls, remain oriented, or can reach an ending. Evaluation should pair observed play with reported experience. | Focuses on serious games and reviews methods rather than testing this platform. The publisher's public preview contains the abstract and bibliographic details; avoid attributing more detailed recommendations to unread sections. | In later playtests, observe first action, control confusion, completion path and error points; follow with short questions about clarity, intended theme and enjoyment. Keep technical metrics (launch and reachable ending) separate from subjective ratings. |

## Synthesis and proposed checks

The prompting studies point to a tension between expressive story writing and enough structure to communicate gameplay constraints. We should preserve an open story field while making a few consequential choices visible and editable. The HCI guidelines add recovery and explanation: when the requested mechanics are simplified or generation fails, the result screen should identify the change and give the author a clear next action. These are design decisions to test, not demonstrated outcomes of this project [1–3].

The game evaluation review broadens planned checks beyond “a file launches.” A first user test can ask a participant to describe a game idea, generate or inspect a representative prototype, identify the simplifications, start playing, perform the intended action, and reach the ending. Record observed completion and confusion separately from the participant's account of whether the game reflects the story [4]. Technical validity, user comprehension and enjoyment are distinct outcomes; no current measurement is claimed.
