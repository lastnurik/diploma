# AI Generation Literature for Story to Game Conversion

This section reviews five research publications relevant to turning a storyline into a playable game. Three are peer-reviewed conference or journal publications; two are research preprints. The studies concern text games, educational games, or Pygame projects rather than this exact proposed 2D browser platform, so their reported success rates are **not forecasts** of our system's performance.

| Source | Approach and evidence | Limitation for our project | Design and evaluation implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **[1] Zhou et al. (2025), STORY2GAME**; research preprint | Generates a story and world, then derives game actions with preconditions and effects; can add actions during play. On a test of 90 dynamically generated actions, the authors report roughly 80% compilation success and roughly 60% semantic success. | Its interface and engine are text-based interactive fiction. The reported action-level result does not establish that a complete 2D browser game is playable, nor does compilation prove that an action has the intended effect. | Represent actions as state-changing rules with explicit preconditions/effects. Check semantic effects and supported mechanics before accepting an action; give the user a simplification or failure explanation for unsupported interactions. |
| **[2] Wang et al. (2023), ByteSized32**; peer-reviewed EMNLP paper | Investigates language-model generation of explicit, executable text-game world models. With 32 example games as a corpus, GPT-4 generated runnable games on unseen topics in 28% of cases in the reported one-shot setup; self-reflection on program errors raised runnability to 58%. The authors propose checks for technical validity, specification adherence and winnability. | These are text games expressed in Python, and the study's prompts, model and corpus differ from a browser engine receiving arbitrary user stories. The rates cannot be transferred directly. | Include a bounded repair loop after deterministic validation, and separately record runnability, fidelity to the requested actions, and whether a player can reach the ending. |
| **[3] Rogosch and Schrader (2026), SINE**; peer-reviewed journal article | A constrained pipeline generates station-based educational interactive fiction from structured seeds using open-weight models, deterministic validation and a repair agent. On a staged evaluation with 240 seeds, finalist configurations reach approximately 68–86% on a combined compilation, playability and learning-goal criterion. | Structured educational seeds and a limited text format are narrower than free-form stories and multiple 2D genres; the combined success criterion includes educational fidelity that our platform does not target. | Use a constrained, machine-readable intermediate specification and validation gates, and report joint success only when the game launches, its key actions work and its goal can be met. Evaluate repairs and failures, not just successful examples. |
| **[4] Agarwal et al. (2026), GameDAI**; peer-reviewed ACL system demonstration | Generates playable educational games from instructor questions using phase-based generation, structured schemas and deterministic mechanic contracts. The authors report 98.3% schema compliance and a 90% validation pass rate on 200 questions across five subject domains. | Inputs are instructor questions, and gameplay is limited to two template families and 15 mechanics. The reported validation pass rate is tied to that constrained configuration, not arbitrary narrative-to-game fidelity. | Separate narrative interpretation, game-specification construction and mechanic validation. Start with a small supported mechanic vocabulary; treat out-of-vocabulary requests as explicit simplification decisions rather than silent partial implementations. |
| **[5] Cai et al. (2026), Spec2Game**; research preprint dated October 2026 | Benchmarks complete Pygame projects generated from detailed natural-language specifications: 15 game families, 150 instances, 14 models and 3,330 generated projects. It evaluates executability, specification realization, code quality and user-facing quality. Across models, launch/execution can be high while rule and termination realization is substantially weaker. | Pygame is a desktop framework, and the inputs are already detailed specifications rather than raw user stories. This is a recent preprint, so its findings warrant independent replication before being treated as settled. | Make goal, allowed actions, state transitions, feedback and ending explicit in the intermediate schema. Test those behaviors individually; a browser window opening is insufficient evidence of a complete game. |

## Critical synthesis

The studies support a staged pipeline rather than a single unconstrained story-to-code step. STORY2GAME [1] shows how preconditions and effects can turn narrative actions into state transitions, while GameDAI [4] and SINE [3] show the practical role of structured contracts, limited mechanics, deterministic checks and repair. The design choice for this diploma project is therefore to extract a **game specification** containing genre, goal, scenes, entities, supported player actions, state changes and win/loss conditions, then run a known browser game client over validated data. This is a proposal derived from the sources, not a measured comparison of alternative implementations.

The evidence also warns against a weak definition of “generated successfully.” ByteSized32 [2] separates runnability from fidelity and winnability. STORY2GAME [1] reports a sizable gap between compiled and semantically correct actions. Spec2Game [5] finds that even complete programs may fail to implement rules and endings. Our acceptance tests should therefore check: (a) valid specification and files, (b) safe browser launch, (c) the requested core action actually changes game state as intended, (d) a reachable ending, and (e) whether disclosed simplifications preserve the user's central premise. The latter needs a documented human review rubric as well as machine checks.

The main unresolved research gap is transfer. None of these papers proves reliable generation of arbitrary user narratives into multiple playable 2D browser genres. Constraining the first version to a supported mechanic set and recording unsupported requests is a defensible scope decision, but its effectiveness must be measured on the team's own varied story corpus and compared against a manually prepared game baseline. This review supplies design hypotheses and evaluation criteria, not project results.
