# Literature Review of Story to Playable Game Generation

The proposed platform must translate a free-form story into a finite 2D browser game with supported actions, an explicit ending, and a clear account of simplifications. This review evaluates four linked problems: interpreting the story, representing game rules, validating playability and safety, and helping non-expert authors understand the output. It uses **14 distinct scholarly sources**: **11 peer-reviewed journal or conference publications**, one scholarly book chapter whose review status was not independently established, and two identified preprints. Thirteen date from 2021–2026; the 2019 human-AI interaction study is retained as a foundational work. No cited experiment directly establishes the success rate of our proposed platform.

## Scope and evidence quality

This is a **preliminary, purposive literature review**, not a systematic review or an exhaustive account of game generation. The selection covers story interpretation, executable game representations, automated validation, browser execution risks, and novice interaction. Sources were retained when they supplied a concrete method, an evaluation finding, or an evaluation framework relevant to one of these problems. Primary publisher records, author-hosted manuscripts and arXiv records were checked on **22 October 2026**. This review does not document a reproducible database search or screening process; a final dissertation review should record search terms, databases, screening decisions and a cutoff date.

The evidence has different roles: L01–L05 address generation; L06–L08 address representation or validation; L09–L10 address adjacent execution risks; L11–L14 inform author interaction and evaluation. Adjacent-domain findings motivate design hypotheses rather than establish game-generator effectiveness. Publication counts describe this selected set; they are not a quality score. Preprint findings remain provisional, small formative studies support exploration, and the percentages below are not directly comparable across papers because inputs, models, outputs and success definitions differ.

## Structured analysis of publications

| Ref. | Approach and evidence | Limitation in relation to our project | Consequence for design or evaluation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **[L01] Zhou et al., STORY2GAME (2025, preprint)** | Derives interactive-fiction actions with explicit preconditions/effects; in its reported 90-action set, compilation success was about 80% and semantic success about 60% (Section V-B). Semantic success was judged by inspecting generated code. | Action-level text-game results neither establish whole-game completion nor transfer to visual 2D browser play. | Represent actions as state transitions and test their effects separately from syntactic validity. |
| **[L02] Wang et al., ByteSized32 (EMNLP 2023)** | Studies executable text-game world models; in its one-shot setup, 28% of generated games ran, rising to 58% after reflection on program errors. The paper distinguishes technical validity, adherence and winnability. | Corpus, model, prompt setting and Python text-game output differ from our use case; those rates are not our forecast. | Use a bounded repair step and separately measure running, requested mechanics and reachable ending. |
| **[L03] Rogosch and Schrader, SINE (2026)** | Uses structured seeds, deterministic validation and repair to generate educational interactive fiction. Finalist configurations report about 68–86% on a joint compilation/playability/learning-goal criterion over a staged 240-seed evaluation. | Constrained educational seed and format are narrower than varied free-form narratives and 2D genres. | Use machine-readable intermediate data and validation gates; report joint task success, not only compilable examples. |
| **[L04] Agarwal et al., GAMED.AI (ACL 2026)** | Phase-based educational game generation with schemas and mechanic contracts; reports 98.3% schema compliance and 90% validation pass on 200 questions within two template families and 15 mechanics. | Limited templates and instructor questions do not establish arbitrary story-to-game coverage. | Bound the mechanic vocabulary; expose unsupported requests as explicit simplifications or failures. |
| **[L05] Cai et al., Spec2Game (October 2026 preprint)** | Benchmarks 3,330 generated Pygame projects from detailed specifications, separating executability, rule/termination realization and user-facing quality. | Desktop Pygame and detailed input specifications are not raw user stories or browser runtime; recent preprint needs independent confirmation. | Put goal, actions, state changes and ending in the schema; a launched window alone is insufficient. |
| **[L06] Martens et al., Ceptre (2024)** | Models mechanics as typed state-transition rules and demonstrates execution traces, simulation and formal queries. | Research language and authored rule sets do not solve automatic story interpretation. | Specify action preconditions/effects and win/loss predicates independently of rendering; reject missing references. |
| **[L07] Facey et al., tile rewrite behaviour trees (2025)** | Uses tile rewrite rules at behaviour-tree leaves to author 2D turn-based games; game-clone case studies and a small formative usability study examine expressiveness and author expectations. | A bounded tile/turn model and small study do not establish coverage of racing or platformer requests. | Implement a known, limited game family and explicit ending semantics before expanding genres. |
| **[L08] Villanueva Aylagas et al., Avalon (2024)** | Uses automated match-3 bot playthrough statistics as feedback for conditional level generation and compares with an ablation. | Match-3 levels and difficulty conditioning differ from narrative generation; results do not quantify our future success. | Check solvability or bounded playthrough separately from schema validity; retain a prewritten baseline game. |
| **[L09] Fernandez-de-Retana et al., browser permissions (2025)** | Empirical study of permission policies and iframe delegation across top websites finds frequent overly broad delegation. | Observational web study, not a security audit of generated games or a validated sandbox configuration. | Treat game output as a trust boundary; grant only required browser capabilities and inspect deployed behavior. |
| **[L10] Hilbig et al., WebAssembly binaries (2021)** | Analyzes 8,461 WebAssembly binaries and host imports, illustrating varied origins and security implications. | Ecosystem snapshot does not compare prospective runtimes for this project. | Do not regard compilation to WebAssembly as sufficient isolation; prefer validated data and a controlled runtime initially. |
| **[L11] Zamfirescu-Pereira et al., novice prompting (CHI 2023)** | Observes ten non-AI experts designing chatbot prompts; participants often generalized from isolated local tests. | Small chatbot study cannot directly predict game-author behavior. | Pair a free-form story field with guided goal, genre and action controls; test how novices interpret changes. |
| **[L12] Kim, Chung and Kim, guided prompting (2026)** | A formative study with 16 novices and a comparative study with 16 participants examine a six-category guided text-to-image interface; reported usability results favor it over Midjourney. | Image creation, small samples and one comparator do not prove a gain for game authoring. | Prototype guided choices with editable narrative; compare against plain story input in later user tests. |
| **[L13] Amershi et al., human-AI guidelines (CHI 2019)** | Synthesizes 18 interaction guidelines, including capability disclosure, correction and graceful failure; evaluation includes 49 design practitioners assessing 20 AI products. | Broad guidance predates this particular game generator and is not a product-specific evaluation. | State supported mechanics; show generation status, reasons for simplification, error/retry and a path to correct the request. |
| **[L14] Kirginas, game UX evaluation (2023)** | Reviews several dimensions and phases of serious-game experience evaluation. | Book chapter is a methods review; available publisher abstract was used conservatively, and no study of our system is claimed. | Observe first actions, control confusion and ending path, then ask about clarity and story fidelity separately from technical metrics. |

## Critical synthesis and design hypotheses

**Expressiveness and reliability are competing aims.** L01 generates new actions to accommodate narratives and player requests, whereas L04 and L07 constrain the available mechanics. L01 also documents failures when an action cannot be represented in its world model. A narrow vocabulary makes validation tractable at the cost of narrative coverage; a generator can produce a technically correct game while removing the author's defining mechanic. Evaluation should record the requested mechanic, the realized mechanic and the disclosed substitution, including refusals for out-of-scope stories. Expanding the vocabulary should be justified by measured coverage and failure patterns.

**A structured contract is useful, but it does not solve the whole problem.** L01 and L06 suggest a way to represent a narrative action as a rule with conditions and effects. L03, L04 and L07 show the value of constrained templates or mechanic vocabularies. Their inputs and game forms are narrow. For this project, the defensible first design is to extract genre, goal, scenes, entities, supported actions, state changes, assets and win/loss conditions into a versioned specification and run it in a controlled browser client. Whether the extraction preserves an arbitrary author's intended story must be evaluated rather than assumed.

**Technical success needs multiple checks.** L01 reports a gap between compiling and semantically correct actions; L02 and L05 distinguish running software from fidelity and winnability. L08 shows why playthrough feedback can matter. Proposed gates are (1) schema and reference validity, (2) safe startup, (3) action/state correspondence, (4) a reachable ending under a bounded search or playthrough, and (5) human review of premise preservation after any simplification. A solver timeout should be recorded as “not verified,” not as proof that a game is impossible. Report denominators, failure classes and repairs, not only favorable examples.

**Safety depends on the execution boundary.** L09 and L10 do not prescribe this project's exact implementation, but they caution against broad browser permissions and untrusted executable output. A constrained specification interpreted by a trusted engine limits what generation can request. If arbitrary generated HTML or JavaScript is introduced later, it will require separate origin/capability review and browser testing. MDN's [iframe guidance](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Elements/iframe) is an implementation reference rather than one of the 14 research publications.

**The author must understand and correct the result.** L11 and L12 motivate a story form with optional structured controls; L13 motivates visible progress, limitations, simplification reasons and retry. L14 reminds us to distinguish a running game from a usable or enjoyable one. A later user study should observe whether authors can state an intended mechanic, recognize a disclosed simplification, launch the game, perform the main action and reach the ending; a short interview can assess whether the premise survived. These are evaluation proposals, not completed user findings.

The principal gap across the reviewed research is transfer to **arbitrary user storylines and several complete 2D browser game genres**. None of the cited works proves that the proposed combination succeeds. The team should start with a bounded mechanic family, test varied stories including out-of-scope cases, compare with a manually prepared game baseline and report failed generations. Platformer, racing and card-game examples may appear in the evaluation corpus without implying that all three are implemented in the first version.

## Source verification and remaining limits

All 14 references below have matching primary publication or author records. Numerical claims in the table were checked against the primary abstract or manuscript, including L01 Section V-B, the L02 and L04 ACL abstracts, the L03 publisher abstract, the L05 arXiv abstract, L10's author manuscript, L12's publisher PDF and L13's author manuscript. This checks attribution, not independent reproducibility of the experiments.

Some publisher pages restrict automated access. For L03, the indexed MDPI abstract and the [authors’ university record](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/automated-generation-and-evaluation-of-interactive-fiction-seriou/) support the summarized method and headline range; the detailed experiment tables were not rechecked in this audit. For L06 and L09–L11, author-hosted copies provide accessible primary evidence alongside the DOI records. L14 is limited to the publisher abstract; its chapter review status and detailed recommendations remain unverified. L01 and L05 are explicitly treated as preprints. These access and evidence limits should be resolved or retained transparently in the final dissertation.
